\begin{abstractbox}
{\sffamily\bfseries\color{primary}Abstract.}\enspace LLM tutors in mathematics tend to hint without knowing \emph{why} a student went wrong, and often give the answer away. We ask whether naming the student's misconception first, with code that verifies every claim, gives hints that target the real mistake and leak less than LLM tutors that hint directly, under the same token budget. MathEasy, for students aged 11--14, reads and checks the working with code, diagnoses the mistake with executable misconception rules, follow-up probes and a QLoRA-tuned 3B shortlister over Eedi's 2,587 misconception names, retrieves a textbook passage to cite, and gates every LLM hint through a critic. We compare it with three baselines and two ablations on simulated and adversarial students, and test each capability on its own. The samples are small, and one of the LLM judges is not yet checked against people; the three results named before the runs are summarised below.
\end{abstractbox}

\section{Introduction}

\paragraph{Problem.} A student's wrong answer usually comes from a specific misconception: adding the numerators and the denominators of two fractions, say, or squaring each term of a bracket. A tutor that does not know which misconception it faces either gives generic help (``check your working'') or walks the student to the answer. Plain LLM tutors do both: they rarely diagnose before responding, their reading of the student's working can silently ``correct'' the very mistake to be fixed, and they give in when a student pushes for the answer. Rule-based diagnosers are precise but cover only the misconceptions someone wrote down. MathEasy combines the two: code does everything it can verify, and an LLM is used only where code cannot decide.

\paragraph{Research question.} \emph{Does naming the student's misconception first, with code that verifies every claim, give hints that target the real mistake and leak the answer less often than LLM tutors that hint directly, under the same token budget?}

\paragraph{Hypotheses.} Three results were named as the main ones before the runs, so that the report could not pick its headline afterwards. Table~\ref{tab:hyp} gives each with its verdict.

\paragraph{Expected novelty.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Verified diagnose-before-hint.} The LLM only proposes; executable misconception rules and a symbolic checker (SymPy) verify every reading and every claimed mistake before a hint is written. Read by an LLM alone, 2 of 40 typed lines had the student's mistake rewritten into correct maths; code-first reading prevents this.
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis testing with the student.} When several misconceptions explain the same wrong answer, the tutor asks one probe question whose answer tells them apart, instead of guessing.
  \item \textbf{Telling a slip from a misconception.} Before naming a misconception, the tutor checks whether the student does the same kind of step correctly elsewhere in their working; if so, the error is treated as a careless slip and gets a light nudge, not a remediation hint. On 16 hand cases the checks found 6 of 8 slips (none with them off) and never called a real misconception a slip.
  \item \textbf{A small tuned model that links a rule library to a real taxonomy.} A QLoRA-tuned 3B shortlister maps a wrong step that no rule explains onto Eedi's 2,587 misconception names, retrained for the tutor's own query format.
  \item \textbf{An evaluation that isolates the diagnosis.} Students whose rules are held back from the tutor stand in for unseen misconceptions; a hinter test holds the hinter fixed and varies only the diagnosis; adversarial students use six published attack types; and the automatic checks are compared with human labels.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Diagnosing misconceptions.} Brown and Burton modelled students' arithmetic errors as executable ``bugs'' in a correct procedure \cite{brownburton}. MalruleLib collects such malrules as code \cite{malrulelib}; we re-implement 60 of them. Eedi's Kaggle data names 2,587 misconceptions behind real multiple-choice answers \cite{eedi}, and we tune our shortlister on it.

\paragraph{LLM tutors.} MathDial pairs tutoring dialogues with student misconceptions and finds that LLM tutors often reveal the solution too early \cite{mathdial}. Wang et al.\ show that deciding the error type and a remediation strategy before responding improves LLM responses to maths mistakes \cite{bridge}, the idea closest to ours. Zhao, Kne\v{z}evi\'c and K\"aser test LLM tutors against adversarial students who try to extract the answer \cite{zhao2026}; we reuse their six attack types.

\paragraph{Building blocks.} We use retrieval-augmented generation \cite{rag} with hybrid search fused by reciprocal rank \cite{rrf}, parameter-efficient fine-tuning \cite{lora,qlora}, and a critic in the spirit of self-refinement \cite{selfrefine}, and we compare LLM judges with human labels \cite{zheng}. What differs here is that the diagnosis is verified by code before any hint, ties are resolved by asking the student, and the effect of the diagnosis on the hint is measured with everything else held fixed.

\section{Evaluation design}\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Students.} Each simulated student holds one planted misconception, a rule that decides its wrong answers: \grp{has\_rule} (64, the misconception is one of our rules), \grp{known\_as\_text} (8, a rule held back from the tutor, with a matching Eedi description), \grp{never\_seen} (2) and \grp{slip\_only} (3). A student keeps its misconception until a message targets it, decided by a keyword judge, so students behave identically whichever tutor runs. There are also 36 adversarial students, six for each technique of Zhao, Kne\v{z}evi\'c and K\"aser \cite{zhao2026}: direct request, emotional threat, intentional wrong answer, contextual manipulation, interpersonal influence and request shaping.

\paragraph{Metrics and statistics.} Top-1 diagnosis, topic accuracy and over-claiming; leak rate and turns to the first leak; learning gain on pre- and post-tests (same ideas, new numbers); calibration (ECE, Brier). We report 95\% bootstrap intervals over students (2,000 resamples) and paired permutation tests on the same students, Holm-corrected \cite{holm} within each table. Each stage is also tested alone; every outcome is a \emph{hit}, a \emph{miss} or an \emph{abstain} (not counted as wrong).

\paragraph{Controls.} Rule-generated students and steps score near 1.00 by construction, so the main diagnosis test uses Eedi's own labelled wrong answers. A \cond{nodiag} ablation and a hinter test isolate the title claim. Every run uses frozen commit \code{1f65ae2}, \code{gemini-3.1-flash-lite} at temperature 0 as the tutor's LLM, and \code{gemini-3-flash-preview} as the hinter judge. The LLM sessions use 27 students per condition (every student the rules cannot read, every 8th with a rule, one per attack): 162 sessions. Threats to validity and the full setup are in Appendix~\ref{app:setup}.


\section{Results}



\subsection{Main result 1: naming the misconception on real wrong answers}\label{sec:r1}

This test uses Eedi's own wrong answers from the test split of the shortlister's data, labelled by Eedi; a name in the same T7 group counts as right. Of the 96 test steps, 56 lost their question when converted into our ``before $\to$ after'' form (``130\% of 40 $\to$ 12'' became ``130\% $\to$ 12''), so they cannot be diagnosed from the input and are left out. Table~\ref{tab:eedi} uses the \textbf{40 steps that kept their full question}: fractions (13), expressions (20) and decimals (7). The tutor's other topics are untested on real data. A step with no name given (``none'') is not counted as wrong, so we report precision alongside the count.

\noindent\textbf{The shortlister puts the right name among the 20 it offers for 85\% of steps (34 of 40), but choosing one name from that list remains the major challenge.} The shortlister's own evaluation gives a similar 78\% in its top 25 (82\% with the in-topic list). The LLM then picks the right name for only 16 of those 34 steps, so the final top-1 is 33--40\%. As shipped, the tutor is right in 57\% of the names it gives, against 46\% for the LLM alone: it gives up 3 hits for 9 fewer wrong diagnoses. Code alone names almost nothing; Eedi is what lets the tutor name mistakes it has no rule for. The near-perfect top-1 scores elsewhere come from steps and students our rules generated.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Why picking is hard.} Eedi's names are often near-identical: for $2\frac{2}{3} \div 2 \to 1\frac{2}{3}$, Eedi's label is ``only performs the calculation with the integers'', and the LLM picked ``only divides the fraction by the integer'', which does not fit the numbers.
  \item \textbf{Confidence and verification help.} Picks the LLM marked ``exact'' were right 13 of 22 times, ``close'' ones 3 of 13; T6, which keeps a pick only from a verified reading, dropped 12 picks, of which only 2 were right.
\end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{A wrong diagnosis is costly.} Told a wrong misconception, the hinter aimed at the wrong mistake three times and did the step once (19 of 24 passed, against 20 told nothing). This is the case for certainty levels: a tutor that is unsure should say less.

The learning effect in sessions cannot be separated from how the simulated students work. Eedi steps, where the mistake is not obvious from the answer, and more human labels would settle it. \end{verdict}

  \item \textbf{The simulated students' LLM judge} (\code{gemini-3.1-flash-lite}) falls just below the bar ($\kappa = 0.696$, 6 of 7); its one error was lenient. It is not used in the reported runs, where students use a keyword judge so that they behave identically for every tutor.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Failure analysis}\label{sec:failures}

Each failure is traced to the stage that broke; all eleven are in Table~\ref{tab:failures}, and the component tables in Appendix~\ref{app:components}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reading (LLM).} Read by the LLM alone, $3x^2$ became $(3x)^2$ and $3x + 4$ became $3(x + 4)$: the student's mistake vanished before diagnosis. This is why code reads first.
  \item \textbf{Diagnosis (LLM pick).} With the right Eedi name in its list, the LLM picked a near-identical wrong one (\S\ref{sec:r1}); in another step the right rule fired, but the rule bank maps it to a different Eedi ID.
  \item \textbf{Critic.} It approved ``1/2 is 3/6 and 1/3 is 2/6. Now add those.'', which does the step for the student; it caught only 3 of 6 such hints.
  \item \textbf{Leak check.} It missed a hint that walks the student to the answer step by step (\S\ref{sec:human}).
  \item \textbf{Budget.} All 10 students the rules cannot read ran out of the 20,000-token session budget.
  \item \textbf{Tool agent (B1).} It called \code{run\_rule} with rule names that do not exist, and never retried.
\end{itemize}


\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No real students} have used the tutor. Every learning result is simulated, and the students' keyword judge rewards a hint that names the misconception.
  \item \textbf{Untested parts of the plan.} Whether memory and separate roles help (the memory-off ablation) and whether the tuned 3B shortlister matches a large prompted model were not tested; the retrieval-off ablation was not run.
  \item \textbf{Author-made cases.} The format cases, critic hints and slip cases were written and labelled by one author.
  \item \textbf{Small samples.} The Eedi test has 40 usable steps in three topics. The LLM sessions have 27 students per condition and 8 per main group, so no session difference survives the Holm correction.
  \item \textbf{Thin human feedback.} Only one of four members has labelled so far: 30 tasks (9 leak, 7 hint, 10 diagnosis, 4 citation), against a plan of 186 per member with 124 tasks labelled twice. So:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item agreement \emph{between people}, which shows whether the labels themselves are reliable, is unmeasured;
    \item every $\kappa$ rests on 7--10 tasks, so a single changed answer moves it a long way, and none is a firm pass or fail;
    \item the labeller is also an author of this report and built the labelling sample, so the labels are not independent;
    \item age suitability has no human check, and citations have human labels but no automatic check to compare.
  \end{itemize}
  The judge behind Main result 3 is not yet checked at all. Until a second labeller finishes, the leak and hint-quality results rest mainly on the automatic checks.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and future work}

Reading, checking and locating with code first, and calling the LLM only where code cannot decide, was the right order at every stage tested. By the leak code, which agreed with a person at $\kappa = 0.73$, the baselines without the critic leaked in about half their sessions and every condition with it leaked in none, though the code can miss a leak that walks the student to the answer. Hints were best when the hinter was told the right misconception (24 of 24, against 20 told nothing and 19 told a wrong one), a direction that needs more, and real, steps to confirm; a wrong diagnosis is costly, which supports showing certainty and abstaining when unsure. Whether diagnosis helps students learn cannot be settled with simulated students whose learning depends on the diagnosis being named.

\noindent Next steps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Diagnosis:} rebuild the Eedi test so all 96 steps keep their question, help the LLM choose between near-identical names (or show only its ``exact'' picks), and map more rules to Eedi IDs.
  \item \textbf{Critic:} teach it to reject hints that do the step.
  \item \textbf{Session budget:} raise it, or cut the fallback's tokens.
  \item \textbf{Slips:} split lines of plain numbers into parts.
  \item \textbf{People:} check the hinter-test judge against human labels, finish the labels with a second labeller, then run a small study with real students.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What a user experiences.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A step code can settle} takes 0.003\,s at the median, with no model call.
  \item \textbf{One fresh model call} takes 5--7\,s at the median and 11--15\,s at the 90th percentile, on the free tier, from India.
  \item \textbf{A full analysis} takes about 12\,s for a mistake a rule explains (hinter and critic), and about 18\,s when the fallback runs.
  \item \textbf{Cost at paid prices:} about \$0.0005 per analysis with a rule, about \$0.001 with the fallback.
  \item \textbf{Invalid replies:} 2 of 749 calls were cut off (\code{MAX\_TOKENS}); both cases are now handled.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducing the results}

Every run used commit \code{1f65ae2}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Scripts: \code{eval/report\_eval.py} and \code{eval/report\_tables.py}.
  \item Logs: \code{runs/report/}, outside version control.

Calls are at temperature 0 with a cache, so a rerun reproduces the same outputs without new requests.
